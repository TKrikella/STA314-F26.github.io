OLS
Consider: Approximately linear relationship; relatively few predictors
Advantage: Simple, interpretable
Limitation: Sensitive to multicollinearity; no unique coefficient estimates when p > n; limited flexibility
Tune: None
Ridge
Consider: Approximately linear relationship; many and/or highly correlated predictors
Advantage: Stabilizes coefficient estimates; accommodates p > n
Limitation: Introduces shrinkage bias; does not perform feature selection
Tune: λ (penalty strength)
LASSO
Consider: Approximately linear relationship; suspected sparsity or interest in feature selection
Advantage: Performs feature selection; accommodates p > n
Limitation: Introduces shrinkage bias; potentially unstable selection of correlated predictors
Tune: λ (penalty strength)
Regularization introduces shrinkage bias in exchange for potentially lower variance.

Regression splines
Consider: Smooth nonlinear relationships
Advantage: Smooth fit with local flexibility
Limitation: Choice of knots; potentially unstable behaviour near boundaries
Tune: Number and location of knots, degree

Natural cubic splines: Impose linearity beyond the boundary knots to reduce boundary instability.
k-NN regression
Consider: Nearby observations have similar responses; low-dimensional data
Advantage: No specified functional form; simple to implement
Limitation: Sensitive to feature scales; curse of dimensionality
Tune: k (number of neighbours)
Local linear regression
Consider: Smooth nonlinear relationships with varying local trends
Advantage: Flexible fit; reduces boundary bias compared with local constant regression
Limitation: Curse of dimensionality; computational cost
Tune: Bandwidth
Splines introduce flexibility through basis expansions, while k-NN and local linear regression make predictions using neighbouring observations.

Pruned regression tree
Consider: Nonlinear relationships; interpretability is important
Advantage: Automatically captures interactions; easy to visualize when small
Limitation: Potentially high variance; observations in the same terminal node receive the same prediction
Tune: Pruning parameter, tree depth
Bagging / Random forest
Consider: Nonlinear relationships; predictive performance is important
Advantage: Averages multiple trees to reduce variance; RF further reduces correlation between trees
Limitation: Less interpretable than a single tree; limited extrapolation
Tune: Number of trees, number of candidate predictors per split (RF)
Boosting
Consider: Nonlinear relationships; predictive performance is important
Advantage: Sequentially fits trees to correct previous prediction errors
Limitation: Less interpretable; can overfit if not appropriately tuned
Tune: Number of trees, learning rate, tree depth
Neural network
Consider: Complex nonlinear relationships; predictive performance is important; large sample size
Advantage: Highly flexible; learns complex relationships
Limitation: Less interpretable; computationally expensive; nonconvex optimization
Tune: Architecture, learning rate, regularization

Logistic regression (binary / multiclass)
Consider: Approximately linear log-odds; interpretation of predictor effects is important
Advantage: Interpretable coefficients; no class-conditional distribution assumptions
Limitation: Linear decision boundaries; no finite MLE under complete separation
Tune: None in basic model
LDA
Consider: Approximately Gaussian class-conditional distributions; common covariance
Advantage: Fewer parameters than QDA; pooled covariance estimate
Limitation: Linear decision boundaries; common covariance assumption
Tune: None in basic model
QDA
Consider: Approximately Gaussian class-conditional distributions; unequal covariances
Advantage: Allows quadratic decision boundaries
Limitation: More parameters; Must estimate a separate covariance matrix for each class; estimates may be unstable when sample sizes within classes are small
Tune: None in basic model
Naive Bayes
Consider: Conditional independence of predictors given class is plausible; many predictors relative to the sample size
Advantage: Simplifies estimation (fewer parameters than QDA); useful in high dimensions
Limitation: Conditional independence assumption may be unrealistic
Tune: None in basic model

k-NN
Consider: Nearby observations tend to belong to the same class; low-dimensional data
Advantage: No specified functional form; flexible decision boundaries
Limitation: Sensitive to feature scales; curse of dimensionality
Tune: k (number of neighbours)
Linear SVM
Consider: Approximately linear decision boundary; class prediction is the primary objective
Advantage: Maximizes the margin; can handle overlapping classes using a soft margin
Limitation: Linear decision boundary; no direct posterior probability estimates
Tune: C (soft-margin cost parameter)
Kernel SVM
Consider: Nonlinear decision boundary; class prediction is the primary objective
Advantage: Captures complex nonlinear decision boundaries
Limitation: Sensitive to feature scales and tuning; less interpretable
Tune: C, kernel choice and kernel parameters
Consider these methods when classification performance is more important than interpreting individual predictor effects, or when we wish to avoid specifying probability models. k-NN and nonlinear kernel SVMs also allow for more flexible decision boundaries.

2. Data
3. Candidate models
4. Training & tuning
5. Selection
6. Evaluation
Which predictors should we include?
Best subset selection
Consider all possible subsets of predictors.
Advantage: Finds the best-fitting subset for each model size
Limitation: Computationally expensive as the number of predictors increases
Forward stepwise selection
Start with no predictors and add one at a time.
Advantage: Computationally cheaper than best subset selection
Limitation: Greedy search; may miss the optimal subset
Backward stepwise selection
Start with all predictors and remove one at a time.
Advantage: Computationally cheaper than best subset selection
Limitation: Greedy search; the full model must be estimable
LASSO
Shrink some coefficients to exactly zero.
Advantage: Simultaneous regularization and feature selection
Limitation: Potentially unstable selection of correlated predictors

Activity 1: Predicting House Prices

A real estate company has collected data on 2,000 houses, including 30 predictors such as square footage, age, location, number of bedrooms and lot size. Some predictors are highly correlated, and exploratory analysis suggests that some relationships with house price are nonlinear.


The company wants to accurately predict the selling price of new listings but would also like to understand which features contribute to higher house prices.


Discuss your thoughts on the following questions with the people around you:

  • Which three models would you consider fitting? Why?
  • How would you compare the candidate models?

Activity 2: Classifying Patients

Researchers collect data on 400 patients, including 15 measurements, to predict whether a patient has a particular medical condition. Approximately 10% of patients have the condition.


Some predictors appear approximately normally distributed within each class, but the researchers are unsure whether the covariance matrices are equal. They also suspect that some relationships may be nonlinear.


Discuss your thoughts on the following questions with the people around you:

  • Which classifiers would you consider? Why?
  • Are there any models that you would be hesitant to fit?
  • How would you compare predictive performance?

Activity 4: Discovering Structure in Customer Data

A company collects 50 measurements on 5,000 customers, describing their purchasing habits. Many of the measurements are highly correlated and are measured on different scales.


The company suspects that there are different types of customers but does not know how many groups exist.


Discuss your thoughts on the following questions with the people around you:

  • Would you apply PCA, K-Means clustering, or both? Why?
  • How would you select the number of principal components/clusters?
  • How would you determine whether the clusters are meaningful?

Final Thoughts

Machine learning is not about memorizing a collection of algorithms or finding the most sophisticated model.

It is about learning to:

  • Ask meaningful questions.
  • Understand the data in front of you.
  • Recognize the assumptions and limitations of your methods.
  • Make informed decisions and justify your choices.


“All models are wrong, but some are useful.” - George Box


If there is one thing you take away from this course, I hope it is not just how to fit a model, but why you would choose to fit it in the first place.
